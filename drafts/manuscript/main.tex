\begin{abstract}
This paper investigates the asset pricing implications of investor-manager interactions on digital platforms. We develop the Effective Sentiment Index (ESI) to combine investor and managerial sentiment within an interactive dialogue. This index extracts a pricing signal by evaluating substantive management optimism conditional on the initial investor state and capturing market concerns exposed by concentrated attention and negative inquiries. Using Q\&A dialogues from Chinese interactive platforms, we find that the value-weighted portfolio strategy that longs high ESI stocks and shorts low ESI stocks earns an annualized risk-adjusted return of about 13.4\% (alpha relative to the augmented q-factor model). Economic mechanism tests show that ESI predicts subsequent earnings surprises and is followed by stronger net buying in large trades than in small trades. Our study sheds light on the pricing effect of the substantive content in the interactions between investors and managers.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

A central question in empirical asset pricing is how investors process corporate information and incorporate it into stock prices \citep{blankespoor2020}. Recent studies use textual analysis to evaluate the information content of investor opinions and managerial disclosures \citep{bartov2018,chen2014,cookson2024,jiang2019}. On the investor side, \citet{chen2014} show that opinions expressed on social media aggregate individual assessments of firm prospects and predict subsequent stock returns and earnings outcomes. On the corporate side, although managers possess private information about expected firm performance \citep{beyer2010financial,healy2001information}, \citet{jiang2019} document that aggregate managerial optimism from corporate filings and conference calls predicts lower future stock returns and negative earnings surprises. They attribute this evidence to extrapolative managerial beliefs and corporate overinvestment. While prior studies establish that investor and managerial text each contains information relevant to stock prices, it remains unclear how the content of a managerial response should be evaluated in relation to the concern that prompted it. A matched dialogue observes both the demand for clarification and the response to that demand. This motivates our question: does the joint interpretation of the two sides contain return information beyond either side in isolation?



In this study, we examine the asset pricing implications of interactions between investors and managers in the Chinese stock market. We argue that the interaction context provides a useful setting for extracting a pricing signal beyond isolated investor and managerial statements. First, interaction connects managerial responses to explicit investor concerns. In traditional disclosures, managers decide the content to disclose based on their expectations of investor demand. In an interactive setting, investor questions identify what the market wants management to explain and can prompt managers to provide clarifications and specific details beyond prepared statements \citep{hollander2010,matsumoto2011}. The information supplied in response can change investors' assessment of firm value \citep{matsumoto2011}. Second, the interaction reveals whether managers address or evade the concern raised. Investors may interpret managerial reluctance or boilerplate replies as negative signals that affect stock prices and market liquidity \citep{hollander2010,xie2023}. Evasive responses can also limit subsequent information production by the media \citep{boustanifar2025}. The matched dialogue therefore reveals both the information supplied in response to a stated concern and management's response choice. These features can produce pricing information beyond either side of the dialogue considered separately.



The Chinese stock market provides an appropriate setting for our study for two main reasons. First, the institutional environment offers a public record of investor-manager interactions. The Shenzhen and Shanghai stock exchanges operate Investor-Manager Interactive Platforms (IIPs) that publicly record matched investor inquiries and corporate replies.\footnote{The Shenzhen Stock Exchange operates Hudongyi (\url{https://irm.cninfo.com.cn/}), and the Shanghai Stock Exchange operates SSE E-Interaction (\url{https://sns.sseinfo.com/}). The exchanges monitor participation and authenticate registered investor accounts. Platform rules prohibit firms from releasing material new information through a reply or editing a question after responding.} These platforms cover almost the entire Chinese stock market, and firms respond to most submitted inquiries.\footnote{\citet{lee2022shall} report that over 99 percent of listed firms had adopted these platforms and that firms respond to over 85 percent of submitted questions.} This comprehensive coverage allows us to observe public interactions across a broad cross section of firms. Second, the Chinese market provides a setting in which to compare information in interactive text with trading pressure associated with investor attention. Retail investors account for a large share of daily trading volume.\footnote{Retail investors account for over 80 percent of daily trading volume in the Chinese equity market \citep{liao2022}.} In addition, strict short-sale constraints prevent arbitrageurs from fully offsetting retail trading pressure \citep{jiang2022}. Retail trading can therefore aggregate into observable stock price movements \citep{bian2026,liao2022}. Despite this substantial retail participation, \citet{carpenter2021} show that Chinese stock prices remain informative about future firm fundamentals. This combination of comprehensive interaction data and specific market frictions allows us to examine whether the textual sentiment extracted from these interactions reflects underlying firm value or temporary price pressure.



To capture the pricing signal from the interactions between investors and managers, we develop the Effective Sentiment Index (ESI). On the investor side, we measure the textual sentiment of investor inquiries to capture retail investors' concerns about firm prospects. On the corporate side, we measure managerial response tone to capture management's view on future fundamentals \citep{jiang2019,li2010}. ESI incorporates three additional features of the interaction. First, prior literature establishes that interaction volume provides independent pricing signals and enhances the predictive power of aggregated opinions \citep{chen2014,cookson2024}. In this case, we weight investor tone by question volume to amplify the pricing signal when attention is highly concentrated. Second, we use reply substantiveness to identify whether managerial tone is accompanied by specific information. \citet{hutton2003} show that positive management forecasts are informative only when accompanied by verifiable statements about future performance. Therefore, we assign less weight to managerial tone for vague replies in the construction of ESI. Third, ESI accounts for the initial investor state. Because affective states influence risk judgments and reliance on advice \citep{gino2012,loewenstein2001risk}, investors process corporate replies differently depending on their preceding sentiment. To capture this dynamic, this index maximizes the weight of managerial tone when investors exhibit a moderately negative state and reduces the weight as sentiment diverges from this point. A high ESI reflects positive investor inquiry paired with an optimistic and substantive managerial reply. We hypothesize that this specific interactive state provides a signal of positive future performance and predicts positive stock returns.


Using IIP records covering 2,489,503 investor questions for 3,600 Chinese A-share firms from 2010 to 2024, our empirical analysis proceeds in two steps. First, we compute sentiment measures from the IIP dialogues. In our sample, investor inquiry tone is mainly neutral but is negatively skewed, with 25.6\% classified as negative and 7.4\% as positive. In contrast, managerial replies are largely neutral (82.7\%) or positive (15.8\%), and only 1.4\% are negative. We also evaluate reply substantiveness and find that 64.4\% of the replies contain substantive information and 35.6\% are classified as boilerplate. Second, we conduct portfolio sorts to evaluate the asset pricing implications of the dialogues. We find that both investor tone and managerial tone predict positive future stock returns. A value-weighted strategy using investor tone earns an annualized return of 10.3\%, and the manager tone strategy earns 6.7\%. We then use conditional double sorts to verify whether these variables interact in the directions implied by our economic motivation. Investor tone predicts returns more effectively when question volume is high. Managerial tone predicts returns mainly when replies are substantive and investor sentiment is moderately negative. Based on our theoretical motivation, ESI combines investor sentiment scaled by attention with managerial tone weighted by reply substantiveness and conditioned on the initial investor sentiment state. A value-weighted portfolio strategy using ESI earns an annualized return of 13.0\% and a Sharpe ratio of 0.93. Its annualized alpha relative to the augmented q-factor model (q5) is about 13.4\%. ESI produces a larger value-weighted return than portfolios using either tone alone. Finally, panel estimates remain significant through 40 trading days, while the Fama-MacBeth coefficients remain positive and significant across the reported horizons.

We next examine the potential economic channels driving this return predictability. First, we find that ESI measured before earnings announcements positively predicts subsequent standardized unexpected earnings. This result suggests that the interactive signal contains forward-looking fundamental information about future profitability. Second, we examine order flow by trade size to proxy for institutional and retail trading. The index predicts stronger net buying in large trades but shows no significant association with small trades. This divergence indicates that institutional investors incorporate this textual signal more efficiently than retail investors. Finally, we investigate the signal around agency conflicts and downside risk to rule out opportunistic managerial behavior. We document that the predictive power of the index remains robust prior to reported insider sales. This persistence confirms that opportunistic promotional disclosures do not drive the observed pricing effect. In addition, higher ESI is associated with lower subsequent stock crash risk. These combined results indicate that substantive public dialogues facilitate the credible release of corporate information rather than serving as tools for strategic manipulation.


Overall, this study makes three contributions. First, we construct an interactive sentiment measure from investor inquiries and managerial replies. Prior studies use textual analysis to extract tone from standalone texts such as social media \citep{bartov2018,chen2014,cookson2024}, financial news \citep{ke2021predicting,lopez2023chatgpt}, or corporate disclosures \citep{jiang2019}. ESI extends this literature by considering investor and managerial sentiment jointly. Its construction also accounts for investor attention, the initial investor sentiment state, and reply substantiveness. The resulting measure provides persistent information about subsequent stock returns. In particular, question volume scales investor tone, while reply substantiveness and the initial investor state determine the weight on managerial tone. This construction makes the dialogue context explicit rather than treating the two texts as interchangeable sentiment observations.

Second, we contribute to asset pricing research by evaluating the pricing value of dialogues between investors and managers. Previous research on interactive platforms focuses on how aggregate participation affects the information environment and corporate decisions \citep{lee2022shall,xu2024}. Our study shifts the focus to stock return prediction and shows that interactive text predicts returns beyond the comparison variables included in our baseline regressions. We compare ESI with investor tone, managerial tone, and their simple average, report both equal- and value-weighted portfolios, and use conditional double sorts to examine the three interaction features entering the index. 

Third, we use the interaction context to refine the interpretation of managerial sentiment. \citet{jiang2019} argue that aggregate sentiment from corporate disclosures reflects managerial overconfidence and extrapolative beliefs. We extend research on managerial sentiment by evaluating managerial tone when listed firms respond to explicit public inquiries. We show that managerial reply tone predicts subsequent returns and that this predictability varies with the preceding investor sentiment state and the substantiveness of the response content.

\section{Literature review}\label{sec:literature_review}

\subsection{Investor sentiment}


Empirical asset pricing studies debate whether investor sentiment introduces transient noise or predicts fundamentals. \citet{delong1990noise} develop a theoretical model to demonstrate that unpredictable changes in noise trader beliefs could move prices away from fundamental values. To study how investor sentiment affects the cross section of stock returns, \citet{baker2006} construct a composite index from market proxies \footnote{The index includes discounts on closed end funds, NYSE turnover, the number of IPOs, returns on the first trading day, the equity share in new issues, and the dividend premium.} and show that high sentiment predicts lower returns, specifically for stocks that are difficult to value and arbitrage. Subsequent research extends these indirect measures to international markets \citep{baker2012global,schmeling2009} \footnote{\citet{baker2012global} construct composite sentiment indices for six international markets and show that global and local sentiment components predict future stock returns. \citet{schmeling2009} uses consumer confidence across 18 countries to measure investor sentiment and predict future returns.} and other asset classes including government bonds, commodities, and index options \citep{baker2012bonds,gao2015,han2008}.\footnote{\citet{baker2012bonds} find that stock market sentiment predicts the excess returns of government bonds. \citet{gao2015} construct a composite index from market proxies using the partial least squares method to explain the returns and comovements of commodity futures. \citet{han2008} show that market pessimism steepens the volatility smile and increases negative skewness for index options.} These indirect measures often capture unrelated market noise, making their predictive power dependent on the choice and aggregation of proxies \citep{huang2015}.

Textual analysis avoids this issue by measuring sentiment directly from expressed opinions rather than market outcomes. Early textual analysis research on investor sentiment aligns with the transient noise perspective. \citet{tetlock2007giving} analyzes a daily market column from the Wall Street Journal and shows that media pessimism predicts price declines followed by reversals, which indicates temporary price pressure instead of fundamental information.


By measuring investor sentiment from text on social media, subsequent studies challenge the transient noise view. \citet{chen2014} analyze Seeking Alpha articles and comments to show that these opinions predict future stock returns and earnings surprises. This evidence suggests that social media platforms can aggregate dispersed information about firm value rather than merely generating random chatter. \citet{bartov2018} find similar results using Twitter data, showing that individual tweets posted before earnings announcements predict forthcoming earnings and returns. Recent research evaluates the economic magnitude of textual signals through portfolio construction. \citet{ke2021predicting} and \citet{lopez2023chatgpt} extract sentiment from financial news to form portfolios and document return predictability. \citet{ke2021predicting} show that the incorporation delay is longer for small stocks. \citet{lopez2023chatgpt} interpret subsequent drift through information processing constraints and limits to arbitrage.

One possible source of these conflicting findings is that sentiment and attention capture distinct dimensions of investor behavior. Traditional market proxies often conflate these two dimensions as they are based on the same aggregate trading outcomes. For example, \citet{baker2006} use market turnover to construct a sentiment index, whereas \citet{barber2008} and \citet{da2011} interpret abnormal trading volume and extreme returns as indirect proxies for investor attention. This overlap implies that trading metrics cannot distinguish the direction of investor sentiment from the intensity of attention or other liquidity factors. Textual data separate these dimensions by using linguistic tone to measure opinions and message volume to measure attention \citep{antweiler2004}. \citet{cookson2024} address this issue by analyzing messages from StockTwits, Twitter, and Seeking Alpha. By separating textual sentiment from message volume, they demonstrate that sentiment contains information relevant to stock prices, whereas attention reflects retail buying pressure and price overreaction.

\subsection{Managerial sentiment}

Managers possess private information about firm prospects, and investors use managerial disclosures to assess firm fundamentals \citep{beyer2010financial,healy2001information}. However, managerial tone can reflect both information and managers' subjective beliefs about firm prospects. On the one hand, tone reflects cognitive biases. Using an aggregate manager sentiment index, \citet{jiang2019} show that optimism predicts lower future aggregate returns and earnings surprises. They also document links between manager sentiment, extrapolation of recent performance, and overinvestment. \citet{chen2018morning} observe that the tone of earnings conference calls becomes more negative later in the day and that investors temporarily overreact to this variation. On the other hand, managerial tone can convey information about future economic conditions. At the macroeconomic level, \citet{demers2024aggregate} demonstrate that positive changes in earnings announcement tone predict economic growth in the following quarter. This evidence suggests that corporate tone also contains information relevant to firm value and future stock returns.

Specific details help investors evaluate whether fundamental information supports managerial tone.  \citet{crawford1982} develop a model to show that conflicts of interest between a sender and a receiver make vague communication unreliable. Since managers have incentives to exaggerate firm prospects, their optimism lacks credibility. In this case, investors tend to place more weight on managerial tone when specific and verifiable information supports the response. Using management earnings forecasts, \citet{hutton2003} report that positive forecasts provide information only when managers include verifiable statements about future performance. \citet{baginski2016verification} analyze management press releases and reveal that quantitative forecast news strengthens the market response to linguistic tone, whereas imprecision weakens the response. 

Managerial communication may also affect how investors perceive firm risk. \citet{gennaioli2015} develop a model of delegated asset management in which trust in fund managers reduces perceived risk and increases investor willingness to hold risky assets. \citet{germann2025trust} test this mechanism in an experimental setting and conclude that greater trust in a fund manager increases confidence and allocations to risky assets. These findings motivate testing whether the return association of managerial replies varies with the sentiment expressed in the preceding inquiry. \citet{loewenstein2001risk} argue that emotional states affect risk judgments. Specifically, anxiety shifts attention toward threats and increases an individual's tendency to seek and rely on external advice \citep{eysenck2007anxiety,gino2012}. These findings suggest that investors evaluate the same managerial reply differently depending on their initial sentiment state.

\subsection{Investor-manager interaction}

Interactive settings may provide corporate information beyond standard disclosures. First, interactive environments reveal additional fundamental details as they allow investors to publicly request specific information \citep{hollander2010}. In traditional disclosures, managers determine the content based on their internal expectations of investor demand. \citet{matsumoto2011} examine over 10,000 earnings calls from 2003 to 2005 to separate prepared presentations from discussion sessions. Using intraday absolute returns, they demonstrate that discussions contain more information than prepared presentations as external questions prompt managers to reveal new details.

Second, the way managers respond to explicit questions provides an additional pricing signal to the market. Response behavior affects prices through direct market reactions and subsequent information production. Investors may directly interpret a refusal to answer as a lack of transparency. \citet{hollander2010} examine individual requests during earnings calls and document that managerial refusal to answer questions is associated with negative intraday stock reactions. Rather than directly refusing to answer, managers often use evasive language to withhold sensitive information \citep{gow2021}. \citet{lee2016} evaluates how managers rely on scripted responses during discussions, reporting that this behavior predicts negative stock returns and lower future operating performance. Similarly, \citet{xie2023} show that boilerplate replies on Chinese platforms occur more frequently during weak firm performance and correlate with reduced market liquidity. Evasive responses can also restrict information production by external intermediaries. \citet{boustanifar2025} report that evasive replies precede less original reporting from professional media. With less substantive content to analyze, reduced media coverage restricts investor engagement and is associated with weaker abnormal returns.


Recent studies examine interactive platforms to understand their market implications. \citet{lee2022shall} analyze approximately 2.5 million investor questions and corporate replies on Chinese IIPs from 2010 to 2017. Using manual annotations and language algorithms, they evaluate the nature of investor questions and measure the level of interaction through platform activity. They find that most questions reflect the difficulties investors face when integrating existing public information. Their results show that greater platform activity is associated with higher market liquidity, narrower trading spreads, and greater price informativeness, suggesting that interactions lower information processing costs. Related research extends these platform activity metrics to corporate outcomes, showing a positive relationship with corporate investment efficiency \citep{xu2024}. 

We construct the Effective Sentiment Index (ESI) from matched investor inquiries and managerial replies on IIPs. The index accounts for the investor's preceding sentiment and whether the managerial response provides substantive information. We then test whether this interaction measure predicts stock returns beyond investor tone and managerial tone considered separately.



\section{Data and descriptive characteristics}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Data and sample construction}

Our primary textual data consist of public Q\&A interactions from the ``Easy Interaction'' platform of the Shenzhen Stock Exchange and the ``E-Interaction'' platform of the Shanghai Stock Exchange. We obtain these records from the Chinese Research Data Services Platform (CNRDS). The robustness analysis uses separate text data from the CNRDS Chinese Listed Company Stock Message Board Comments Database, which contains posts from GUBA.

We obtain stock returns, prices, trading variables, financial statements, and listing dates from the China Stock Market and Accounting Research (CSMAR) database. We merge these data with the Q\&A records by firm and trading date. Each observation corresponds to one firm on one trading day. The baseline descriptive sample requires a valid daily stock return and excludes observations from the first six months after listing. Return regressions additionally require nonmissing textual signals, return outcomes, and the controls included in the specification. The mechanism tests require the corresponding earnings, order flow, holdings, or crash risk outcome.

The resulting sample contains 5,812,275 observations at the firm-day level and covers 3,854 firms from 2010 through 2024. Active Q\&A interactions occur in 1,317,473 observations, accounting for 22.67\% of the sample. These active observations contain 2,489,503 investor questions for 3,600 firms. Investor tone, manager tone, reply substantiveness, and ESI are defined only when an interaction occurs. We do not assign zero values to these variables on days without an interaction since the absence of a dialogue is not a neutral textual signal. Figure \ref{fig:iip_adoption} reports the annual number of active firms and investor questions.





\paragraph{Matching a question to its reply and assigning the observation date.}
Replies can arrive after the question, so the date to which a dialogue is assigned must be distinguished from the date on which the reply becomes public. We retain the question--reply links supplied in each platform record. We then group the linked records by firm and question date. Investor tone is averaged over the questions in that group; managerial tone and reply substantiveness are aggregated from their corresponding replies. Thus the two inputs are aggregated separately within the same matched question-date cohort. They are not independently pooled from all questions and all replies posted by a firm on the same calendar date.

For example, if a question is posted on Day 1 and its linked reply arrives on Day 3, the original construction assigns both text inputs to the Day 1 cohort. The reply does not become a second, unrelated Day 3 input merely because it is posted on that date. If a firm receives several questions on Day 1, their own linked replies enter that cohort even when those replies arrive on different dates. A reply to an earlier question belongs to that earlier question-date cohort. ESI requires its tone, substantiveness, and attention inputs; a missing input is not filled with a neutral zero.

This convention measures the content of the completed dialogue retrospectively. In the original question-date O2C tests, a reply arriving on Day 3 was not necessarily observable before the Day 2 return. The timing checks in Appendix Table \ref{tab:horizon_robustness} and the separately labeled public-availability C2C topic supplement distinguish dialogue attribution from information availability. The original question-date return results are retained; the date convention should not be interpreted as making a late reply publicly observable earlier.

\subsection{Construction of ESI}\label{sec:esi_construction}

The construction has three steps. First, score the linked question and reply, and aggregate their scores within the firm--question-date cohort. Second, summarize attention by question volume and scale managerial tone using its sample distribution. Third, combine the attention-weighted investor component with managerial tone weighted by reply substantiveness and the initial investor state. The equations below describe these weights; Appendix Table \ref{tab:parameters} reports the distributional quantities used to implement them.


We use separate BERT classifiers for investor inquiries and managerial replies \citep{liu2020finbert}. Each classifier assigns probabilities to negative, neutral, and positive sentiment. We define the continuous tone score as the probability of positive sentiment minus the probability of negative sentiment. The score therefore ranges from minus one to one. When a firm has multiple inquiries or replies on the same day, we use the mean score for that text source. This approach preserves the direction and confidence of the classification, which is useful as words can have different meanings in financial text \citep{loughran2011}.

We measure reply substantiveness with a Random Forest classifier. The classifier is trained on 2,000 interactions with manual labels. Its inputs include question and reply lengths, textual similarity between the question and reply, indicators for numbers, dates, and percentages, and phrases associated with generic disclosure language. The continuous score equals one minus the estimated probability that a reply is evasive. Higher values therefore indicate replies with more specific content. Appendix \ref{app:cases} presents examples with low and high substantiveness scores.

The platforms supply matched inquiries and replies, which we aggregate to the firm and trading day. ESI measures the signed interaction signal on that day. A simple sum of the two tone scores would assign the same importance to a reply regardless of the attention to the inquiry, the content of the reply, or the investor's initial sentiment. ESI allows these features to affect the contribution of each side of the interaction.

Let $I_{i,t}$ denote investor tone and $M_{i,t}$ denote managerial tone. As described above, each tone equals the predicted probability of positive sentiment minus the predicted probability of negative sentiment. Investor text can contain information about future returns and earnings \citep{bartov2018,chen2014,cookson2024}. We therefore use $I_{i,t}$ as the signed investor component.

We use question volume as a proxy for investor attention towards a firm. Prior research highlights the importance of attention in shaping the subset of stocks investors evaluate, noting that spikes in retail attention predict buying pressure and short-run price dynamics \citep{barber2008,da2011}. Building on this insight, we assume that heightened attention amplifies the market impact of investor sentiment. 

To capture this amplifying effect, we define normalized attention as:

\begin{equation}
A_{i,t}=\frac{\log(1+N_{i,t})}{\max_{j,s}\log(1+N_{j,s})},
\end{equation}
\noindent where $N_{i,t}$ is the number of investor questions for firm $i$ on day $t$. 

We then incorporate this metric into the overall investor component as $I_{i,t}(1+\lambda A_{i,t})$. This specification ensures that question volume scales the magnitude of the investor signal without altering the directional sign of the underlying tone.

Managerial tone can reflect information about firm prospects and managerial beliefs \citep{jiang2019}. Its contribution is less clear when a reply contains generic language. Prior research shows that the market response to managerial language depends on whether it is supported by verifiable details and whether the reply is evasive or generic \citep{boustanifar2025,hutton2003,xie2023}. We standardize managerial tone as $Z^M_{i,t}=(M_{i,t}-\overline{M})/\sigma_M$ and multiply it by the substantiveness probability $Sub_{i,t}$. This step gives less weight to managerial tone when the reply contains little specific content.

Prior research shows that affective states influence risk judgments and reliance on advice \citep{gino2012,loewenstein2001risk}. We therefore allow the contribution of a managerial reply to vary with the sentiment expressed in the preceding inquiry. We use investor tone itself as the state variable, so $State_{i,t}=I_{i,t}$. Rather than impose a discrete cutoff, we translate the distance between investor tone and a moderately negative anchor into a continuous state weight. Asset pricing studies use kernels to give more weight to observations close to the point at which a conditional quantity is evaluated \citep{aitsahalia1998,ang2012testing}. Dynamic asset pricing models also implement Gaussian kernels when estimating time-varying exposures \citep{adrian2015regression}. We apply the same distance weighting principle to investor tone. The Gaussian kernel is nonnegative and symmetric \citep{silverman1986density}. Its weight declines smoothly with the squared standardized distance from the anchor. The symmetric form treats deviations on either side of the anchor comparably because the existing evidence does not identify different rates of decline. The weight remains positive for all finite investor tone values, which avoids assigning zero weight solely because investor tone crosses an arbitrary cutoff. The kernel determines only the relative weight on managerial tone and does not assume that investor tone follows a normal distribution. The state weight is
\begin{equation}
K(I_{i,t})=\exp\left[-\frac{(I_{i,t}-\theta)^2}{2(b\sigma_I)^2}\right].
\end{equation}
\noindent The state weight $K(I_{i,t})$ equals one when investor tone is at $\theta$ and declines continuously as investor tone moves away from that value. The density normalizing constant is omitted because $K(I_{i,t})$ is a relative weight rather than an estimated probability density. Let $q$ denote the anchor quantile and let $\theta=Q_I(q)$ denote the corresponding investor tone value. In the baseline specification, $q=0.30$ represents the moderately negative investor state, $\sigma_I$ is the standard deviation of investor tone, and $b=0.8$ controls the rate at which the weight declines. Appendix Table \ref{tab:grid_search_summary} varies $q$ and $b$ and replaces the Gaussian kernel with Epanechnikov and uniform kernels \citep{epanechnikov1969}.

Combining these inputs, we define
\begin{equation}
G_{i,t}=Z^M_{i,t}\times Sub_{i,t}\times K(I_{i,t})
\end{equation}
\noindent where $G_{i,t}$ is the managerial component. It combines standardized managerial tone with reply substantiveness and the state weight.
\noindent The final index is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:esi}
ESI_{i,t}=I_{i,t}(1+\lambda A_{i,t})+\gamma G_{i,t}.
\end{equation}
\noindent We set $\lambda=0.5$ and $\gamma=5$ in the baseline specification.

These constants have distinct roles. With normalized attention between zero and one, $\lambda=0.5$ allows the investor component to rise from its unscaled value to 1.5 times that value. The constant $\gamma=5$ sets the relative scale of the standardized managerial component after substantiveness and state weighting. The anchor $q=0.30$ places the peak state weight at the 30th percentile of investor tone, and $b=0.8$ sets the kernel bandwidth to 0.8 investor-tone standard deviations. Neither the kernel nor this quantile choice assumes normally distributed tone. These are the retained baseline design constants, not estimates of structural economic parameters or a claim that a unique optimal specification has been identified.

The centering, dispersion, quantile, and attention normalization use the full original 2010--2024 text pools. They do not use a 2010--2018 training window. Because the original regression and portfolio preparation rules yield different eligible pools, their distributional scales are reported separately in Appendix Table \ref{tab:parameters}; the constants remain the same. Figure \ref{fig:tone_kde} and Table \ref{tab:summary_stats} describe the underlying distributions, while Appendix Table \ref{tab:grid_search_summary} retains the original sensitivity results. Full-sample normalization is part of this historical specification and is not an out-of-sample calibration exercise.\footnote{The original implementation also suppresses a negative managerial contribution when investor tone is positive: $\widetilde G=0$ if $I>0$ and $G<0$, and $\widetilde G=G$ otherwise, with $\widetilde G$ used in the implemented index. This implementation convention is retained with the historical results; no parameters or observations are changed in this revision.}
 A higher ESI indicates a more positive interaction signal after accounting for attention, reply substantiveness, and the investor sentiment state.

\subsection{Variables and descriptive statistics}

Our primary dependent variable is the next-day open-to-close (O2C) excess return. We calculate the stock return as the closing price divided by the opening price minus one, and then subtract the daily risk-free rate. This timing excludes the overnight interval between the interaction date and the next market open. It focuses the outcome on the next intraday interval; the question-date assignment described above separately determines whether the complete dialogue was already public \citep{aboody2018,lopez2023chatgpt}.

We control for the public information environment. Following \citet{chen2014}, we compute a news coverage indicator ($Newsdummy_{t-30,t-3}$) and average news sentiment ($Newstone_{t-30,t-3}$) over a trailing window that ends three trading days before the interaction.

We update accounting controls using the most recent financial report available before day $t$. These variables include firm size ($Size$), the book-to-market ratio ($BM$), leverage ($Lev$), profitability ($ROA$), and log firm age ($\ln(\textit{FirmAge})$). We winsorize continuous characteristics at the 1st and 99th percentiles.

The trading variables include the Amihud illiquidity measure ($Illiqd$) \citep{amihud2002illiquidity}, turnover ($Dturn$), and prior return measures. We use large-trade order imbalance ($OIB^{inst}$) as a proxy for institutional trading and small-trade order imbalance ($OIB^{ind}$) as a proxy for retail trading, based on transaction direction and trade size cutoffs \citep{lee1992,lee1991inferring,tong2013}. We define $OIB^{diff}$ as $OIB^{inst}-OIB^{ind}$ for the paired coefficient comparison in Section \ref{sec:microstructure_oib}. The earnings tests use standardized unexpected earnings ($SUE$) \citep{bernard1989post}. Appendix Table \ref{tab:operating_income_sue} also reports adjusted earnings per share and unexpected operating income. The remaining diagnostic outcomes are mutual fund ownership change, subsequent insider selling, NCSKEW, and DUVOL. Appendix \ref{app:variable_definitions} reports the definitions, calculations, and data sources for these variables. The factor series described above are used to estimate factor-adjusted returns and portfolio alphas.

Table \ref{tab:summary_stats} reports summary statistics for the main variables. The baseline sample contains 5.81 million observations at the firm-day level. The continuous substantiveness score has a mean of 0.582 and a median of 0.638. ESI has a mean of 0.486 and a median of $-0.077$ among observations with active interactions.

Table \ref{tab:textual_coverage} reports sample coverage and categorical textual outcomes. Negative sentiment accounts for 25.6\% of investor inquiries, while positive sentiment accounts for 7.4\%. Managerial replies are mainly neutral at 82.7\% or positive at 15.8\%. For this descriptive table, replies with a substantiveness probability of at least 0.5 are classified as substantive. This convention classifies 64.4\% of replies as substantive. The empirical tests use the continuous probability.

Figure \ref{fig:tone_kde} plots the continuous tone distributions. Investor tone is more negative and more dispersed, while managerial tone is concentrated near neutral and positive values. The difference shows why the interaction cannot be represented by either tone alone. Investor inquiries reveal concerns about firm prospects, while managerial replies provide a distinct assessment of those concerns.

Figure \ref{fig:sentiment_evolution} plots the monthly averages of investor and managerial tone. Investor tone declines during the 2015 to 2016 market crash, the 2018 trade dispute, and the 2022 market downturn. Managerial tone remains more stable and less negative during these periods. The two series therefore capture different sides of the communication process over time.

Table \ref{tab:correlation_matrix} reports Pearson and Spearman correlations among the main textual, market, and firm variables. Investor tone and managerial tone have low correlations with most firm characteristics and with public media sentiment ($Newstone_{t-30,t-3}$). The correlation between managerial tone and reply substantiveness is larger, which is consistent with more specific replies using different language than generic replies. The return tests below assess whether ESI contains predictive information after controlling for these variables.
\subsection{Content and question nature classification}\label{sec:topic_classification}

The completed pipeline uses Qwen3 text embeddings and supervised classification with a fixed taxonomy. It is not an unsupervised discovery of latent topics. Content is classified separately for questions and replies; question nature is classified on the question side. The full archive contains 5,836,158 paired source records, including 5,836,155 nonempty questions. \section{Baseline return predictability}\label{sec:baseline_return_predictability}
 

\subsection{Portfolio sorts}

To evaluate the return predictability of interactive communication, we form daily portfolios sorted based on each textual signal. To eliminate noise from neutral signals, we require long candidates to exhibit positive tone and short candidates to exhibit negative tone for directional measures. In this case, we first restrict the long and short candidate pools to the upper and lower 30 percent of the daily signal distribution. Following the portfolio design in \citet{ke2021predicting}, we select up to 50 firms with the most extreme values in each leg. The fixed number keeps portfolio breadth comparable across days with different levels of platform activity. Firm-days without an available interaction are excluded from the ranking. We also exclude stocks that reach a price limit on the execution day. The primary return is the O2C excess return on day $t+1$. We report equal weight (EW) and value weight (VW) long minus short returns, annualized performance, and alphas from the CH4 and q5 models.\footnote{CH4 is the China factor benchmark following \citet{liu2019}. The historical q5 series is the U.S. Global-q augmented q-factor model following \citet{hou2021}; it is not a Chinese CSMAR q5 series. We retain the original q5 results as a separate comparison rather than relabeling their geography. A domestic FF3 comparison is a distinct specification.}


Table \ref{tab:horse_race} reports the performance of portfolios sorted on ESI and the alternative textual signals. Under equal weighting, the ESI portfolio earns 5.32 bps per day, equivalent to an annualized return of 12.9\%, and attains a Sharpe ratio of 1.54. Its q5 alpha is 5.43 bps ($t\text{-stat} = 6.08$). Under value weighting, the ESI portfolio earns 5.35 bps per day, or 13.0\% annually, with a q5 alpha of 5.50 bps ($t\text{-stat} = 3.74$) and a Sharpe ratio of 0.93. For comparison, investor tone produces q5 alphas of 4.56 bps under equal weighting and 4.41 bps under value weighting. The corresponding managerial tone alphas are 2.46 and 2.69 bps. Aggregate Tone, defined as the simple average of investor and managerial tone, produces a q5 alpha of 2.87 bps under equal weighting and an insignificant 1.24 bps under value weighting. ESI therefore produces the largest q5 alpha among the textual signals under both weighting methods. This comparison motivates the conditional sorts that follow, which examine the interaction characteristics incorporated into ESI.

Table \ref{tab:conditional_double_sorts} examines the conditional associations that enter ESI. The dependent double sorts test whether the return spread associated with one tone changes across levels of another interaction characteristic. Panel A conditions on reply substantiveness and then sorts on managerial tone. The q5 alpha is negative in Q1 and Q2, but positive in Q4 and Q5. The difference between Q5 and Q1 is 6.58 bps ($t\text{-stat} = 2.47$). This result associates the return signal in managerial tone with the substantive content of the reply.

Panel B conditions on investor tone and then sorts on managerial tone. Managerial tone produces positive alphas in Q2 and Q3, whereas the estimates in Q1, Q4, and Q5 are indistinguishable from zero. These results reveal that the pricing effect of managerial tone concentrates in situations where initial investor sentiment is moderately negative. While optimistic investors require no reassurance and extreme panic clouds rational judgment, investors in a moderately negative state retain both the demand for information and the capacity to evaluate corporate replies logically. This dynamic aligns with the theoretical framework in \citet{gennaioli2015}. In their model, anxious investors seek financial experts to reduce perceived risk and increase their willingness to hold risky assets. A parallel mechanism operates in our interactive setting. Moderately concerned investors seek clarification from corporate managers. Optimistic managerial responses function as expert advice that reduces perceived risk and restores investor confidence. Consequently, the initial investor state determines the effectiveness of the managerial signal.

Panel C conditions on question volume and then sorts on investor tone. The investor tone alpha reaches 6.14 bps in the highest volume quintile. The Q5 minus Q1 difference is 8.10 bps ($t\text{-stat} = 3.18$), consistent with evidence that attention affects the pricing of investor sentiment \citep{barber2022attention,da2011}. These conditional associations correspond to the substantiveness weight, investor state weight, and attention term in Equation (\ref{eq:esi}).

\subsection{Return predictive regressions}

Portfolio sorts do not simultaneously condition on several firm characteristics. We therefore estimate the following panel OLS model:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rpr}
\begin{aligned}
 R_{i, t+1 \to t+h} = \alpha + \beta_1 ESI_{i,t} + \boldsymbol{\delta}'\mathbf{X}_{i,t} + \mu_i + \tau_t + \varepsilon_{i, t+1 \to t+h}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $R_{i,t+1 \to t+h}$ is the cumulative q5-adjusted excess return for firm $i$ from trading day $t+1$ through horizon $h$, with $h \in \{1, 5, 10, 20, 40, 60\}$. The control variables $\mathbf{X}_{i,t}$ include firm size ($Size_{i,t}$), the book-to-market ratio ($BM_{i,t}$), return on assets ($ROA_{i,t}$), leverage ($Lev_{i,t}$), Amihud illiquidity ($Illiqd_{i,t}$), daily turnover ($Dturn_{i,t}$), the preceding stock return ($Ret_{i,t-1}$), return volatility ($Vol_{i,[t-30,t-1]}$), prior cumulative return ($Ret_{i,[t-30,t-2]}$), historical media tone ($Newstone_{i,[t-30,t-3]}$), and a news coverage indicator ($Newsdummy_{i,[t-30,t-3]}$). The coefficient vector on these controls is $\boldsymbol{\delta}$. Firm and date fixed effects are denoted by $\mu_i$ and $\tau_t$.

Panel A of Table \ref{tab:panel_horse_race} reports the panel OLS estimates. A one standard deviation increase in ESI predicts 1.77 bps over one day ($t\text{-stat} = 5.48$). The coefficient rises to 6.07 bps over ten days ($t\text{-stat} = 5.38$) and remains significant over twenty days. It is marginally significant over forty days at 5.54 bps ($t\text{-stat} = 1.95$). The estimate falls to 3.97 bps ($t\text{-stat} = 1.06$) over sixty days, when statistical significance disappears. Investor tone becomes insignificant at twenty days. Managerial tone becomes insignificant at forty days. The panel estimates therefore show a longer predictive horizon for ESI than for either tone considered separately.

Panel B reports Fama and MacBeth regressions \citep{fama1973risk}. We estimate a cross-sectional regression on each trading day and average the resulting coefficients across days. The ESI coefficient is positive at every horizon and significant at the 10 percent level or better. It rises from 2.00 bps over one day ($t\text{-stat} = 1.86$) to 13.48 bps over forty days ($t\text{-stat} = 3.03$), then declines to 10.93 bps over sixty days ($t\text{-stat} = 2.21$). Investor tone is significant only at the one-day horizon. Managerial tone predicts returns between five and forty days, but lacks predictive power over horizons of one and sixty days. The Fama and MacBeth estimates provide a cross-sectional check on the panel results.

Table \ref{tab:horse_race} also reports CH4 and q5 alphas from time-series regressions of the ESI portfolio return on standard factor returns. Appendix Table \ref{tab:ols_robustness_matrix} compares raw excess returns with FF3, CH4, and q5 adjustments across holding horizons. These specifications examine whether standard factor models absorb the ESI return pattern.

Taken together, the panel OLS and Fama and MacBeth estimates show that ESI positively predicts future excess returns and remains significant over longer horizons than investor tone or managerial tone considered separately. These estimates establish the baseline return association, while leaving open whether ESI reflects future fundamental news or subsequent trading.

\section{Microstructure frictions and economic mechanisms}\label{sec:mechanisms}

While Section \ref{sec:baseline_return_predictability} establishes return predictability, it does not reveal whether ESI is associated with future fundamentals or which transaction size groups respond to the signal. We therefore examine subsequent earnings surprises and compare the order imbalance of large and small trades. These analyses assess whether the pricing effect connects to future profitability and trading by different order size groups. To evaluate the vulnerability of the signal to strategic manipulation and its relation to tail risk, the final two subsections explore an observable agency conflict window and subsequent stock crash risk.

\subsection{The fundamental information channel}\label{sec:fundamental_channel}

We first investigate whether ESI captures fundamental information rather than temporary price pressure from retail investors. Since an informative signal should anticipate future profitability, we follow \citet{chen2014} and regress subsequent Standardized Unexpected Earnings (SUE) on ESI measured before the historical earnings-event date.\footnote{The retained Annual Review SUE results use the source update date as the event reference. That date is not necessarily the actual announcement date (\texttt{Annodt}). References to announcement timing in the inherited specification describe its original intended interpretation; these coefficients should not be presented as a verified true-announcement-date test. This revision preserves the historical table and does not replace a true-announcement-date result with it.}

Analyst coverage in the Chinese market is not uniformly distributed, particularly among smaller firms. We therefore use a seasonal random walk model to measure earnings surprises rather than relying on analyst consensus forecasts \citep{bernard1989post}. Due to mandatory interim reporting requirements in China, we conduct our analysis at a biannual frequency. We construct the Standardized Unexpected Earnings ($SUE$) metric as follows.

\begin{equation}
    SUE_{i,h} = \frac{EPS_{i,h} - EPS_{i,h-2}}{\sigma_{i,h}}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $EPS_{i,h}$ represents the reported earnings per share for firm $i$ in period $h$. The numerator captures the unexpected EPS based on a seasonal random walk model. We calculate the difference between current earnings ($EPS_{i,h}$) and earnings from the corresponding period in the previous year ($EPS_{i,h-2}$). This subtraction removes strong seasonal variations inherent in corporate operational cycles. The resulting difference represents the fundamental surprise. We adjust this value for changes in total share capital, such as stock splits, to maintain consistency per share. The denominator $\sigma_{i,h}$ represents the historical standard deviation of this unexpected EPS calculated over the preceding five periods.

To test whether platform interactions predict these future fundamental surprises, we estimate the following regression:

\begin{equation}
    SUE_{i, h} = \alpha + \beta_1 ESI_{i,[t_{h-30}, t_{h-3}]} + \boldsymbol{\delta}'\mathbf{X}_{i,h-1} + \mu_i + \tau_h + \varepsilon_{i,h}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $SUE_{i,h}$ is the earnings surprise for firm $i$ in period $h$, and $t_h$ is the historical earnings-event date described above. $ESI_{i,[t_{h-30},t_{h-3}]}$ is the average ESI over 28 trading-day observations ending three trading days before the announcement. This window excludes the final two trading days to reduce overlap with information released immediately before the announcement. $\mathbf{X}_{i,h-1}$ includes lagged SUE, the cumulative return over the same window, and firm controls. The coefficient vector on these controls is $\boldsymbol{\delta}$. Firm and reporting period fixed effects are denoted by $\mu_i$ and $\tau_h$.

Table \ref{tab:fundamental_sue} reports the results. The coefficient on aggregated ESI is 0.065 ($t\text{-stat}=2.49$). We also estimate the same model separately for investor tone and managerial tone on the ESI sample. Investor tone has a coefficient of 0.209 ($t\text{-stat}=6.82$), while managerial tone has a coefficient of 0.027 ($t\text{-stat}=1.14$). The earnings association is therefore concentrated in the investor component. These separate regressions do not establish that ESI contains incremental earnings information beyond investor tone.

Appendix Table \ref{tab:operating_income_sue} reports two alternative earnings outcomes. The ESI coefficient is 0.012 ($t\text{-stat}=1.28$) for adjusted EPS after controls. It is 0.001 ($t\text{-stat}=0.34$) for unexpected operating income scaled by lagged assets. Neither estimate is statistically significant. We therefore treat these tests as limits on the fundamental interpretation rather than as supporting evidence.

\subsection{The microstructure order flow channel}
\label{sec:microstructure_oib}

Return predictability must eventually be reflected in trading. We therefore examine which transaction size groups exhibit net buying after the interaction signal. Following \citet{lee1992} and \citet{tong2013}, we use trade size as an imperfect proxy for investor type. Large and extra-large orders form the institutional proxy. Small and medium orders form the retail proxy.\footnote{The classifications follow the CSMAR transaction data. Small orders are below RMB 50,000. Medium orders are at least RMB 50,000 and below RMB 200,000. Large orders are at least RMB 200,000 and below RMB 1,000,000. Extra-large orders are at least RMB 1,000,000.} For each proxy, daily order imbalance is buy volume minus sell volume divided by total trading volume. We also define the paired difference as $OIB^{diff}=OIB^{inst}-OIB^{ind}$ before estimation. Large and small order groups are size-based proxies rather than observed investor account identities. The five-day outcome below is the mean daily imbalance over days $t+1$ through $t+5$, not a cumulative imbalance. Accordingly, we estimate the panel regression for each order type and horizon as follows:

\begin{equation}
    OIB^{Type}_{i, t+k} = \alpha + \beta_1 ESI_{i,t} + \boldsymbol{\delta}'\mathbf{X}_{i,t} + \mu_i + \tau_t + \varepsilon_{i, t+k}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $OIB^{Type}_{i,t+k}$ is the order imbalance for firm $i$ on day $t+1$ or the average over days $t+1$ through $t+5$. $Type \in \{inst,ind\}$ denotes the large order and small order proxies. $\mathbf{X}_{i,t}$ includes the contemporaneous return, rolling volatility, turnover, and firm characteristics. The coefficient vector on these controls is $\boldsymbol{\delta}$. Firm and trading day fixed effects are denoted by $\mu_i$ and $\tau_t$.

To compare the two order groups, we estimate the same specification using the difference in order imbalances ($OIB^{diff}_{i,t+k} = OIB^{inst}_{i,t+k} - OIB^{ind}_{i,t+k}$) as the dependent variable.\footnote{The signal coefficient from this difference regression equals $\beta^{inst} - \beta^{ind}$. The standard error clustered by firm and date retains the covariance between the paired order imbalance outcomes. Following the multiple equation testing framework in \citet{zellner1962} and the coefficient comparison method in \citet{lou2019}, we apply a Wald test to evaluate the null hypothesis $H_0: \beta^{inst} = \beta^{ind}$.} Panel A of Table \ref{tab:microstructure_oib} reports the order flow results. The index predicts positive large order imbalance on day $t+1$ with a coefficient of 0.095 ($t\text{-stat}=8.67$). The estimate equals 0.066 ($t\text{-stat}=10.49$) over days $t+1$ through $t+5$. The corresponding coefficients for small orders are indistinguishable from zero. The coefficient difference for the index is 0.100 ($t\text{-stat}=8.63$) on day $t+1$ and 0.066 ($t\text{-stat}=9.68$) over the subsequent five days. The corresponding differences equal 0.138 ($t\text{-stat}=11.36$) and 0.053 ($t\text{-stat}=7.64$) for investor tone, and 0.072 ($t\text{-stat}=6.21$) and 0.054 ($t\text{-stat}=7.56$) for managerial tone. These positive differences show that large trades exhibit a greater response to textual signals than small trades. Given the trade size proxy, this pattern is consistent with institutional investors responding more strongly to textual signals \citep{boehmere2021}. The test does not identify investor type at the account level.
 
Panel B uses mutual fund ownership as an alternative measure of institutional holdings. The dependent variable is the quarterly change in shares held by mutual funds relative to shares outstanding. Its coefficient on lagged quarterly ESI is 0.076 ($t\text{-stat}=3.99$). This association is consistent with mutual funds increasing ownership after high ESI periods. 




\subsection{Agency conflicts: The stress test}

As ESI incorporates managerial replies, this positive signal might reflect strategic optimism rather than information about future fundamentals. This credibility concern becomes more salient before insider sales. Managers then have an incentive to inflate the stock price. We therefore test whether the predictive power of the index declines prior to reported insider transactions. Following the communication framework in \citet{crawford1982}, we use this setting to evaluate communication under a known conflict of interest without identifying the underlying motive for each specific reply.

The insider transaction data come from the CSMAR insider shareholding change records. We define $Sell^{Next30d}_{i,t}$ as one when the data report any negative insider share change during the next 21 trading days (30 calendar days). We estimate the following interaction model:

\begin{equation}
    Ret_{i, t+1} = \alpha + \beta_1 ESI_{i,t} + \beta_2 Sell^{Next30d}_{i,t} + \beta_3 (ESI_{i,t} \times Sell^{Next30d}_{i,t}) + \boldsymbol{\delta}'\mathbf{X}_{i,t} + \mu_i + \tau_t + \varepsilon_{i, t+1}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $Ret_{i,t+1}$ is the next-day O2C excess return in basis points. $\beta_1$ is the ESI coefficient outside the insider sale window. $\beta_3$ measures the difference in ESI predictability when an insider sale is reported during the next 21 trading days. $\mathbf{X}_{i,t}$ contains the baseline controls. Firm and date fixed effects are denoted by $\mu_i$ and $\tau_t$.

Panel A of Table \ref{tab:diagnostic_tests} reports the results by specification. Column (1) estimates the baseline relation between ESI and next-day O2C excess returns in this sample. The ESI coefficient is 1.78 bps ($t\text{-stat}=5.60$). Column (2) adds the insider sale indicator. The ESI coefficient remains 1.78 bps ($t\text{-stat}=5.60$), while the coefficient on the sale indicator is 1.29 bps ($t\text{-stat}=1.01$) and is not statistically significant. Column (3) allows ESI predictability to differ inside the sale window. The ESI coefficient of 1.86 bps ($t\text{-stat}=5.67$) measures the association outside the sale window. The interaction coefficient of $-1.08$ bps ($t\text{-stat}=-0.93$) measures the change inside the sale window. Their sum is 0.78 bps ($t\text{-stat}=0.67$), which is the total ESI coefficient when a subsequent insider sale is observed. Neither the interaction coefficient nor the total coefficient is statistically significant. This test therefore provides no evidence that ESI predictability is significantly weaker before reported insider sales.

\subsection{Subsequent crash risk}

The baseline tests concern average returns. We next ask whether the interaction signal is also associated with the left tail of the return distribution. This extension is motivated by the bad news hoarding explanation of stock price crashes. When managers withhold adverse information, accumulated news can enter prices abruptly and produce negative return skewness \citep{hutton2009,kim2011}.

We aggregate ESI to the firm-quarter and examine two crash risk measures in the next quarter. NCSKEW is the negative coefficient of skewness, and DUVOL is the down-to-up volatility ratio. Both measures are constructed from weekly stock returns within a quarter. We regress each measure in quarter $q+1$ on the average ESI in quarter $q$, firm controls, firm fixed effects, and time fixed effects.

Panel B of Table \ref{tab:diagnostic_tests} reports a coefficient of $-0.021$ ($t\text{-stat}=-4.42$) for NCSKEW and $-0.022$ ($t\text{-stat}=-4.48$) for DUVOL. Higher quarterly ESI is therefore associated with lower measured crash risk in the following quarter. These results show that the interaction signal contains information related to subsequent downside outcomes.


\section{Robustness checks} \label{sec:robustness_checks}

This section examines whether ESI predictability depends on portfolio concentration, index parameters, return measurement, sample period, or broader retail discussion. Each test addresses a specific design choice in the baseline analysis. The corresponding results appear in the appendix.

Portfolios weighted by market capitalization would assign disproportionate allocations to the largest firms. To prevent the idiosyncratic price movements of a few large companies from dictating portfolio performance, we cap the allocation for any single stock at 10 percent of its portfolio leg. Appendix Table \ref{tab:vw_capped_horse_race} reports that the return difference persists at 6.02 basis points under this cap, accompanied by a q5 alpha of 6.19 basis points. The result reduces concern that a few large firms account for the baseline return spread.

We next span the parameters and measures applied to ESI. Appendix Table \ref{tab:grid_search_summary} changes one parameter at a time. It evaluates $\gamma \in \{1,2,3,5,7,10\}$, $\lambda \in \{0,0.2,0.5,0.8,1\}$, $b \in \{0.4,0.6,0.8,1,1.2\}$, and $q \in \{0.10,0.20,0.30,0.40,0.50\}$. The baseline values are $\gamma=5$, $\lambda=0.5$, $b=0.8$, and $q=0.30$. Appendix Table \ref{tab:kernel_robustness} replaces the Gaussian kernel with Epanechnikov and uniform kernels \citep{epanechnikov1969}. It also replaces the tone and substantiveness inputs with word count measures. The ESI estimates remain positive across these alternatives, although their magnitude varies. These tests show that the baseline estimate is not produced by one isolated parameter value or kernel choice.

Appendix Table \ref{tab:horizon_robustness} examines return timing and portfolio design. The baseline O2C return is measured from the open to the close on day $t+1$. O2O is measured from the open on day $t+1$ to the open on day $t+2$. C2C is the daily close-to-close return reported for day $t+1$. The table also varies the number of stocks in each portfolio leg across 30, 50, 70, and 100. The ESI long-minus-short estimates remain positive under these choices. Appendix Table \ref{tab:ols_robustness_matrix} reports cumulative return regressions through 20 trading days using raw, CH4, q5, and FF3-adjusted returns \citep{fama1993common,hou2020replicating,liu2019}.

GUBA provides a separate measure of retail discussion outside the matched investor and manager dialogues. We add daily GUBA net tone and posting activity to test whether ESI only captures broader retail discussion.\footnote{The GUBA data come from the Chinese Listed Company Stock Message Board Comments Database of Chinese Research Data Services (CNRDS). GUBA (\url{http://guba.eastmoney.com/}) is an online message board operated by East Money. Retail investors use this website to discuss specific stocks and post market opinions.} In the full control O2C regression, the ESI coefficient declines from 1.67 to 1.40 bps and remains statistically significant (Appendix Table \ref{tab:guba_robustness}, Panel A). Panel B compares market-adjusted GUBA tone and posting activity over days $+1$ through $+3$ with their averages over days $-3$ through $-1$ around a management reply. Both measures decline after the reply. Reply timing is endogenous and event windows overlap, so we treat these changes as descriptive rather than causal.

We split the sample at 2020 to assess whether the coefficient is concentrated in the earlier platform period or the period following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. The ESI coefficients are 1.36 bps for 2010 through 2019 and 1.37 bps for 2020 through 2024. This comparison is descriptive and does not impose a formal structural break.

Finally, we apply a mechanical cost of 2 bps per unit of portfolio turnover. The net one-day return is 2.42 bps and remains statistically positive. The corresponding three-day and five-day estimates are $-2.52$ and $-3.61$ bps. The longer holding strategies therefore do not survive this cost assumption. This calculation is not a complete implementation model, but it identifies turnover as an important limit on the economic value of the strategy.





\subsection{Exploratory return slopes across content themes}\label{sec:topic_slopes}

The completed content classification also permits an exploratory examination of whether the ESI slope differs across the concerns raised by investors. This supplement uses the existing public-availability C2C specification, rather than reinterpreting the original question-date O2C estimates as the same test. Text inputs are dated after the matched replies become available. The outcome is the next-day close-to-close excess return; it is not a CH4-residualized return. The supplement retains its own previously frozen historical calibration, including $\lambda=0.4811$, $\gamma=3.0495$, and $b=0.7425$, rather than substituting the original baseline constants. Thus differences from the main results can reflect timing, return definition, calibration, and sample eligibility as well as content.

For availability-date firm observations, let $D^k_{i,t}$ identify the dominant question-content category. Dominance is determined by the largest category count, including unclassified records. With unclassified or low-score content as the reference category, we estimate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:topic_slopes}
\begin{split}
r^{C2C,excess}_{i,t+1}={}&\mu_i+\tau_t+\beta_0 ESI^z_{i,t}
+\sum_{k=1}^{19}a_kD^k_{i,t}\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{19}\delta_k(ESI^z_{i,t}D^k_{i,t})
+\boldsymbol{c}'\mathbf X_{i,t}+\varepsilon_{i,t+1}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Here $ESI^z$ is standardized with the retained historical scale, and $\mathbf X$ contains the supplement's controls. A second specification additionally includes investor and managerial tone. Firm and date fixed effects are included, with standard errors clustered by firm and date. The category-specific slope is $\beta_0+\delta_k$; the reference slope is $\beta_0$.

Appendix Table \ref{tab:full_topic_heterogeneity} reports all category slopes in basis points per ESI standard deviation. Products or business has a positive slope of 2.02 ($t=3.40$) in the specification without the two separate tones. The slope is 1.12 ($t=1.25$) after both tones are added. The BH $p$ column reports Benjamini--Hochberg multiple-testing adjustments for all category slopes in that latter specification, rather than a test of differences across categories. The separate joint Wald test evaluates $H_0:\delta_1=\cdots=\delta_{19}=0$; its $p$-values are 0.865 and 0.873. These tests do not reject equal slopes across themes. Accordingly, this content analysis adds descriptive interpretation and exploratory estimates to the original results, without claiming that the strongest individual slope establishes a different return mechanism.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This study examines whether direct dialogues between corporate managers and retail investors provide incremental information about subsequent stock returns. Using Q\&A records from Chinese IIPs from 2010 to 2024, we construct ESI. This index integrates investor tone, managerial tone, attention, reply substantiveness, and the initial investor sentiment state.

Our empirical analysis documents the return predictability of the Effective Sentiment Index (ESI). First, we show that portfolio strategies weighted by market capitalization based on ESI earn significant annualized returns. ESI significantly outperforms unconditioned aggregate tone metrics by accounting for initial investor states and reply substantiveness. Panel regressions further confirm that this interactive signal persists over extended horizons. These tests illustrate that evaluating managerial optimism conditional on investor expectations could extract pricing information from public dialogues.

Second, we provide evidence on the economic mechanisms driving this predictability. We find that ESI is positively associated with subsequent earnings surprises, indicating that the interaction signal captures fundamental information prior to formal accounting releases. Furthermore, high ESI signals are followed by significant net buying from institutional investors and subsequent increases in mutual fund holdings. In contrast, retail transaction proxies exhibit no such positive net buying. This asymmetric order flow confirms that sophisticated institutional capital actively incorporates these textual signals into stock prices.

Third, we stress test the signal against agency conflicts and downside risk. We find no evidence that the predictive power of the ESI degrades or reverses around subsequent insider selling, confirming that opportunistic promotional disclosures do not drive our results. Moreover, higher ESI accompanied by greater reply substantiveness is associated with less severe left-tail outcomes at both daily and quarterly frequencies. This finding suggests that substantive interactions effectively mitigate extreme crash risk by facilitating the gradual release of corporate information.

These findings contribute to the literature on textual analysis, managerial sentiment, and corporate disclosure \citep{chen2014,jiang2019}. We advance the literature by evaluating paired, interactive text rather than isolated investor or manager tone. We demonstrate that managerial optimism predicts stock returns most effectively when replies contain substantive details and are evaluated in the context of the initial investor sentiment state. Overall, our study shows that direct interactive communication provides information related to subsequent price formation in financial markets.

\section{Textual analysis methodology} \label{app:nlp_methodology}

To support replication, this appendix describes the natural language processing procedures used to construct investor tone, managerial tone, and reply substantiveness. These variables provide the text inputs to ESI. Dialogues on IIPs differ from annual reports and news articles as they are short, tied to a specific request, and often contain informal Chinese expressions, financial abbreviations, and firm specific references. General sentiment dictionaries are not well suited to this setting since financial words can have domain specific meanings and dictionary methods do not reliably account for context, negation, or the relation between a question and its reply \citep{loughran2011}. We therefore use supervised models trained on financial text rather than a general dictionary measure.

To train the text classifiers, we use 2,000 manually labeled Q\&A interactions. Investor inquiries and managerial replies are labeled as negative, neutral, or positive. Replies are also labeled according to whether they are evasive. One observation has a missing evasiveness label, leaving 1,999 usable interactions for the substantiveness classifier. We train separate BERT classifiers for investor and managerial sentiment \citep{liu2020finbert}. For each firm and day, we aggregate investor tone and managerial tone separately. We train a Random Forest classifier for evasiveness and define reply substantiveness as one minus the estimated probability of an evasive reply.

The Random Forest inputs include the lengths of the question and reply, the reply to question length ratio, character level TF-IDF cosine similarity, indicators for boilerplate phrases, and indicators for numbers, dates, and percentages. For model assessment, we use an 80\% training sample and a 20\% test sample with a random seed of 42. The classifier contains 150 trees with a maximum depth of 10. The production model is fitted to the available labeled observations. Its output is converted into a continuous substantiveness score by subtracting the estimated probability of an evasive reply from one. The continuous score allows ESI to reduce the contribution of managerial tone gradually rather than applying a binary cutoff.

\paragraph{Comparison on a common holdout.}
To compare the substantiveness measurement across model families, we retain the completed benchmark using the same 402 labeled test pairs for Random Forest, Chinese BERT, and a local instruction language model. This benchmark uses a template-separated split of the 1,999 usable labeled pairs, with 1,280 training and 317 validation pairs; it is distinct from the original production fitting procedure above. Table \ref{tab:substantiveness_benchmark} reports discrimination and calibration measures. BERT performs better on these existing labels, but this comparison does not change the production Random Forest scores used in the original return tables. Independent semantic validation remains a separate task.

  \textit{GUBA Log Posts} & The natural logarithm of one plus the total number of GUBA posts for a firm on day $t$. The event comparison subtracts its daily cross-sectional mean. & CNRDS & - \\
  \addlinespace
  \textit{GUBA Active} & An indicator equal to 1 when the firm has at least one GUBA post on day $t$, and 0 otherwise. & CNRDS & - \\
\end{xltabular}

\section{Examples of platform dialogue across different scenarios} \label{app:cases}

This appendix presents representative Q\&A examples categorized by the interaction patterns identified in our text analysis. 

\subsection{Scenario A: High manager sentiment and low investor sentiment}
\textit{Characteristics: High investor anxiety (Investor Tone $< -0.9$) countered by optimistic managerial reassurance (Manager Tone $> +0.9$).}
\vspace{0.3cm}

\noindent \textbf{Example 1} \\
\textbf{格林美} [Stock Code: 002340]. \textit{Scores: Inv Tone: -0.94, Mgr Tone: +0.96}. \\
\textbf{Question:} Hello Secretary! I heard that your company battery project has been losing money and the stock price is overvalued. What is your opinion? (董秘您好！听说贵公司的电池项目一直亏损，股价严重高估，您怎么看？) \\
\textbf{Reply:} Thank you for your attention! The company is engaged in the production of precursor products for new energy vehicle power batteries and power battery recycling... It is an industry leader in the field of power battery recycling. The company subsidiaries... have been included as pilot enterprises in the MIIT ``white list'' (ranking first in the industry with 3 included sites)... Market share has reached historical peaks... which will become a new growth pole for the company. (感谢您的关注！公司从事新能源汽车动力电池用前驱体产品生产和动力电池回收...将成为推动公司业绩增长的新增长极。)
\vspace{0.4cm}

\noindent \textbf{Example 2} \\
\textbf{皇台酒业} [Stock Code: 000995]. \textit{Scores: Inv Tone: -0.93, Mgr Tone: +0.96}. \\
\textbf{Question:} Hello Secretary, the stock has been close to the lower trading limit recently. Is it because the liquor business is suffering large losses? If the management is poor, what measures do you have? (你好董秘，这几天股票连续接近跌停，是因为公司的白酒业务亏损严重吗？...公司有什么措施？) \\
\textbf{Reply:} Dear investor! Secondary market price fluctuations are affected by many factors. The company will strictly follow the 2021 business plan... focusing on the strategic awakening of the brand ``Moutai in the South, Huangtai in the North''... establishing two strategic core product lines... to achieve the revival of the famous liquor brand. (投资者您好！二级市场股价变动受多种因素的影响...打造经典皇台系列、窖底原浆系列两条战略核心产品线...实现企业整体竞争力的提升。)
\vspace{0.6cm}

\subsection{Scenario B: Positive investor tone and positive managerial tone}
\textit{Characteristics: Low retail concern with managerial optimism.}

\vspace{0.3cm}

Revenue from shipbuilding, repair, and marine engineering was RMB 34.446 billion, an increase of 22.39\%. Management also cites a shift toward vessels with higher value added and stable orders during the recovery of the shipping and shipbuilding industries. (感谢您的关注...取得了较好的接单成果，打开了长期可持续发展的空间。)

\subsection{Scenario C: Low manager sentiment}

These conditions reduced new projects and project margins. Financial distress among several automaker customers also increased receivable impairments. Based on its 2022 earnings forecast, the company expects net profit for 2022 to be negative. (尊敬的投资者，您好！近年来公司业务占比最大的汽车制造业面临了芯片短缺、原材料价格持续高位、现金流等不利因素影响。自2018年四季度下游汽车行业低迷，行业出现汽车整车厂新生产线投资项目暂缓或停止的情况，新增产线项目大幅减少，行业竞争激烈，导致公司签订的项目毛利率大幅下降公司业绩受到影响，尤其是2021年下半年包括南京知行汽车、宝沃汽车、华晨汽车、宝能汽车等在内的部分国内汽车整车制造厂商或进入被申请破产流程或申请破产或出现重大经营风险，导致公司报告期内的应收款项减值大幅增加。根据《江苏哈工智能机器人股份有限公司2022年度业绩预告》，预计公司2022年度净利润为负值。感谢您对公司的支持!)

\subsection{Scenario E: High substantiveness (hard information)}
